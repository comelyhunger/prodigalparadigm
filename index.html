What I Do

Organizations buy AI tools. Then they discover that buying the tool and actually deploying it are two entirely different problems. The vendor leaves. The team is uncertain. The technology sits.

I close that gap. I work with teams to build the scaffolding that makes AI agents actually work in production — the knowledge bases, the prompt architecture, the feedback loops, the human processes that turn a brilliant but amnesiac inference engine into a reliable colleague.

The quality of your agent depends on what you hand it, not how smart it is.

About the Name

I've been called prodigal, and it wasn't meant as a compliment. So I did what I always do with words: looked it up. The first definition is the accusation — wasteful, recklessly extravagant. But the second is a philosophy: "yielding abundantly; lavish. Nature has been so prodigal of her bounty." That's the definition I built a company on.

Well-stocked systems give lavishly — so lavishly it can look like waste — and the strange arithmetic is that they get better by giving. People do too.

A paradigm is the framework everyone works inside without noticing, right up until the anomalies pile high enough to force a shift. Prodigal Paradigm is the shift I help organizations make: from scarcity operating models to well-stocked ones — teams with enough margin, enough skill, and enough captured knowledge that generosity becomes the default instead of the exception.

The prodigal, you'll remember, came home. The interesting part is what got learned on the way back.

Before I bought the name, I checked its provenance — collector's habit. The only prior academic use of the phrase turned out to be a research paper proposing solutions to data security challenges. The name was already doing the work. I just gave it an office.
